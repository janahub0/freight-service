# Urban Climate Adaptation and Sustainability Reporting: Study Notes

These notes cover two related topics: how cities cope with extreme rainfall, and how companies report on sustainability. They were written as a practice document for retrieval experiments, so every answer in them can be checked by reading the text.

## Why Cities Flood

Cities flood more easily than rural areas because so much of the ground is covered by impervious surfaces such as asphalt, concrete and rooftops. Rain that would soak into soil in a field instead runs straight off these surfaces and into drains. Storm drains and sewers are designed for a certain capacity, and when rainfall is more intense than that design capacity, water backs up and spills onto streets. Short, very intense bursts of rain are especially dangerous because they arrive faster than the drainage network can carry the water away. Older neighbourhoods often have combined sewers that carry both rainwater and wastewater, which means overloaded pipes can also cause pollution. Low-lying streets, underpasses and basements are the places that flood first.

## Types of Flooding

Flooding is usually grouped by where the water comes from. Pluvial flooding happens when heavy rain overwhelms the ground and drainage systems directly, without any river overflowing. It is the typical flash flood in a city street. Fluvial flooding happens when a river bursts its banks, usually after prolonged rain upstream or snowmelt. Coastal flooding is caused by storm surges, high tides and rising sea levels pushing seawater inland. Groundwater flooding occurs when the water table rises above the surface after a long wet period. A single event can involve more than one type, for example heavy rain in a coastal city during a storm surge.

## Sustainable Drainage and Sponge Cities

Sustainable drainage systems, known by the acronym SuDS, manage rainwater close to where it falls instead of sending it all into pipes. Common SuDS features include green roofs, permeable paving, rain gardens, swales (shallow grassed channels), and retention ponds that hold water temporarily. The sponge city idea, associated with Chinese urban planning, applies the same principle at city scale: parks, wetlands and permeable surfaces are designed to absorb, store and slowly release rainwater. These nature-based solutions reduce peak runoff, filter pollutants, and often cool the surrounding area. Their main limits are space, soil type, and maintenance, since a blocked swale or clogged paving stops working.

## Rainfall Nowcasting and Early Warning

Nowcasting means forecasting rainfall a few minutes to a few hours ahead. It relies on weather radar, rain gauges and satellite data, and increasingly on machine learning models that learn patterns from past events. A flood early warning system turns these predictions into alerts when rainfall is expected to cross a threshold. Machine learning models for heavy rainfall are often trained on data from one city or region. A common problem is generalisation: a model that performs well in the city it was trained on can perform noticeably worse in another city, because climate, topography and sensor coverage differ. Testing a model on a city it has never seen is therefore a more honest measure of quality than testing on held-out data from the same city.

## Urban Heat Islands

An urban heat island is a city area that is noticeably warmer than the surrounding countryside, especially at night. Dark surfaces absorb and store heat during the day, buildings block airflow, and there is less vegetation to cool the air through evapotranspiration. Waste heat from vehicles and air conditioning adds to the effect. Heat islands raise energy demand, worsen air quality, and increase heat-related health risks, particularly for older people. Trees, green roofs and reflective surfaces are common ways to reduce the effect, and they overlap with the drainage measures used against flooding.

## What ESG Means

ESG stands for environmental, social and governance. The environmental pillar covers topics such as greenhouse gas emissions, energy use, water and biodiversity. The social pillar covers employees, working conditions, human rights in the supply chain, and communities. The governance pillar covers how a company is run: board structure, executive pay, ethics and anti-corruption policies. Investors, regulators and customers use ESG information to judge risks and opportunities that do not show up in ordinary financial statements. ESG ratings are produced by third-party agencies, and they often disagree with each other because each agency uses different methods and weights.

## CSRD, ESRS and Double Materiality

The Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive, abbreviated CSRD, is European Union legislation that requires companies in scope to publish detailed sustainability information. The reporting content is defined by the European Sustainability Reporting Standards, abbreviated ESRS. A central idea is double materiality: a company must report on how sustainability issues affect the company financially (financial materiality) and also on the company's impacts on people and the environment (impact materiality). A topic is reportable if it is material from either perspective. The scope and timeline of the CSRD have been revised more than once, so the current legal text should always be checked before relying on a deadline.

## Greenhouse Gas Scopes

The Greenhouse Gas Protocol divides a company's emissions into three scopes. Scope 1 covers direct emissions from sources the company owns or controls, such as company vehicles and on-site boilers. Scope 2 covers indirect emissions from purchased electricity, heat and steam. Scope 3 covers all other indirect emissions in the value chain, both upstream (purchased goods, business travel, transport) and downstream (use and disposal of sold products). For most companies Scope 3 is the largest share of total emissions and also the hardest to measure, because it depends on data from suppliers and customers.

## The EU Taxonomy

The EU Taxonomy is a classification system that defines which economic activities can be called environmentally sustainable. An activity qualifies if it makes a substantial contribution to at least one of the EU's environmental objectives, such as climate change mitigation or adaptation, does no significant harm to the other objectives, and meets minimum social safeguards. Companies in scope report what share of their revenue, capital expenditure and operating expenditure is taxonomy-aligned. The purpose of the Taxonomy is to give investors a common definition and to make it harder to label activities as green without evidence.

## Greenwashing

Greenwashing means presenting a company, product or investment as more environmentally friendly than it really is. Typical warning signs include vague claims such as "eco-friendly" or "planet-positive" with no evidence, highlighting one small green feature while ignoring a much larger harmful impact, carbon-neutral claims that rely mostly on offsets instead of real emission cuts, and impressive-looking labels or logos that were invented by the company itself. Claims backed by a recognised standard, third-party verification, and specific measurable targets are more trustworthy. Regulators in several jurisdictions now take enforcement action against misleading environmental claims.

## Carbon Accounting Data Quality

Carbon accounting turns activity data, such as litres of fuel burned or kilowatt-hours of electricity used, into emissions by multiplying it with an emission factor. Emission factors come from published databases maintained by governments, research bodies and industry groups, and they differ by country, year and fuel or energy mix. Data quality varies a lot. Primary data measured directly at the source is the most reliable, while spend-based estimates, which multiply money spent by an average factor, are rough and can hide big differences between suppliers. Good practice is to document the data source, the emission factor and its year for every number, so that the result can be audited and improved over time.
